Chapter Overview

Civil liberties are constitutionally guaranteed protections against arbitrary government interference. This interactive module explores the Bill of Rights, selective incorporation via the Fourteenth Amendment, freedom of religion and expression, rights of the accused, and zones of constitutional privacy.

Origins & Federalists vs. Antifederalists

Module 4.1

Hamilton argued a Bill of Rights was unnecessary given delegated powers, while Antifederalists demanded written checks against federal overreach, leading to the 1791 ratification of the first 10 amendments.

Target: ratify_bill_of_rights --year=1791 --amendments=10

Selective Incorporation & 14th Amendment

Module 4.2

Barron v. Baltimore (1833) originally held that the Bill of Rights applied only to federal actions. Beginning with Gitlow v. New York (1925), selective incorporation used the 14th Amendment's Due Process Clause to apply protections to state governments.

Freedom of Religion: Establishment Clause

Module 4.3

The Establishment Clause prevents the federal government from establishing an official state religion or favoring one faith over another, enforcing a "wall of separation" tested in Lemon v. Kurtzman and Carson v. Makin.

Module 4.4

Guarantees citizens the right to practice any religion or non-belief. Landmarked in WV Board of Education v. Barnette (refusing flag pledge) and Burwell v. Hobby Lobby (corporate religious exemptions).

Political, Symbolic & Student Speech

Module 4.5

Political speech holds the highest protection under strict scrutiny. Student armbands were protected in Tinker v. Des Moines (1969), while explicit anti-drug rules limited speech in Morse v. Frederick (2007).

Freedom of Press & Prior Restraint

Module 4.6

Prior restraint prevents government censorship before publication, allowed only in extreme threats (Near v. Minnesota). Libel (written) and slander (spoken) require proving "actual malice" for public figures.

Second Amendment: Individual Gun Rights

Module 4.7

District of Columbia v. Heller (2008) recognized an individual constitutional right to keep firearms at home for self-defense, which was incorporated against states in McDonald v. Chicago (2010).

Fourth Amendment & Exclusionary Rule

Module 4.8

Protects against unreasonable search and seizure. Mapp v. Ohio (1961) established the Exclusionary Rule (illegally seized evidence is inadmissible), extended to digital tracking in US v. Jones (2012).

Right to Privacy & Modern Jurisprudence

Module 4.10

Privacy zones stem from constitutional penumbras (Griswold v. Connecticut 1965). Established key frameworks for birth control, abortion (Roe v. Wade 1973, later overturned in Dobbs v. Jackson 2022), and private consensual relations (Lawrence v. Texas 2003).

Substantive vs. Procedural Due Process

Module 4.11

Procedural due process ensures government acts fairly and follows legal steps before taking life, liberty, or property. Substantive due process checks whether the laws themselves are fair and constitutional.

The Lemon Test (Lemon v. Kurtzman)

Module 4.12

Government aid to religious schools is constitutional only if: 1) it has a secular purpose, 2) its primary effect neither advances nor inhibits religion, and 3) it avoids excessive government entanglement.
